Burkitt lymphoma

Why this disease is important

In Africa Burkitt lymphoma makes up almost half of childhood non-Hodgkin lymphoma, and Epstein-Barr virus is in virtually every endemic tumour.

Burkitt lymphoma is among the fastest-growing human tumours.

Burkitt lymphoma shrinks fast on chemotherapy and can be cured. In Africa long-term cure is uncommon, because poor supportive care limits how intensive treatment can be.

Consider this case

A 7-year-old boy is brought with a swelling of the left jaw that has grown over four weeks. His teeth on that side are loose, and his left eye is pushed forward. He's had malaria many times.

On examination there's a firm, non-tender mass of the left jaw, left proptosis, and a mass in the right side of the abdomen.

Investigations: an aspirate of the jaw mass shows sheets of uniform, medium-sized cells with vacuolated cytoplasm. A biopsy shows a starry-sky pattern. The tumour cells are CD20, CD10 and BCL6 positive and TdT negative, and Ki-67 stains nearly every cell. EBER in situ hybridisation is positive in the tumour cells. An HIV test is negative. Plasma LDH is raised.

He starts cyclophosphamide, vincristine and methotrexate, with methotrexate into the CSF, and allopurinol and intravenous fluids. The next day his potassium, urate and phosphate are high, his calcium is low and his creatinine is rising.

Gross morphology of Burkitt lymphoma

Key facts

  • Endemic Burkitt tumours often grow in the maxilla or mandible and distort the face fast.
  • Sporadic Burkitt lymphoma more often involves the bowel, the retroperitoneum and the ovaries.
  • In endemic areas the abdomen is now involved more often than the jaw.

Burkitt lymphoma grows as a mass in the organ it arises in, and the organs differ between the forms.

Histology of Burkitt lymphoma

Key facts

  • Burkitt cells are uniform and medium-sized (intermediate), with round nuclei, small nucleoli and basophilic cytoplasm.
  • Tingible body macrophages eat apoptotic tumour cells and form the pale stars of the starry sky.
  • Tingible body macrophages are benign host cells, not tumour cells.

Formation of the starry sky in Burkitt lymphoma

  1. MYC keeps the tumour cells cycling, so Burkitt lymphoma grows faster than almost any other human tumour. Many mitoses are seen.

  2. Alongside the division there's a very high rate of . Dying cells break up and leave fragments of nuclear debris among the living ones.

  3. Benign tissue macrophages engulf the nuclear debris. These are host cells, not tumour.

  4. Each macrophage has pale cytoplasm and often sits in a clear space. Against the dark, basophilic tumour cells it stands out.

  5. At low power the pale macrophages, scattered through the dark sheet of tumour cells, look like stars in a night sky.

Watch out. A starry sky doesn't prove Burkitt lymphoma. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and B-lymphoblastic leukaemia can show one too. The immunophenotype and the MYC translocation confirm the diagnosis.

Aetiology of Burkitt lymphoma

Virtually every Burkitt lymphoma carries a MYC translocation. What differs is the cofactor: Epstein-Barr virus and malaria in the endemic form, HIV in the immunodeficiency form, and usually none known in the sporadic form.

Remember. A MYC translocation isn't a cofactor: every form of Burkitt lymphoma carries one. Epstein-Barr virus, malaria and HIV each raise the chance that a dividing B cell gets it.

Pathogenesis of Burkitt lymphoma

Key facts

  • In endemic Burkitt lymphoma, Epstein-Barr virus and malaria keep B cells dividing, and a MYC translocation in one of them starts the tumour.
  • Sporadic Burkitt lymphoma usually has no Epstein-Barr virus but reaches the same MYC translocation.

Endemic Burkitt lymphoma builds up in steps. Epstein-Barr virus makes B cells divide, and malaria weakens the T cells that control them. Then one mistake in a dividing cell switches on MYC.

Pathogenesis of endemic Burkitt lymphoma, step by step

Step 1 of 4

EBV and malaria keep B cells dividing

Epstein-Barr virus drives infected B cells to divide, and repeated malaria weakens the T cells that would kill them, so a large pool of dividing B cells builds up.

A break in the DNA

In the germinal centre, B cells cut and rejoin their immunoglobulin genes during class switching and . These steps go wrong easily. A wrong join can move MYC from chromosome 8 next to the heavy-chain gene on chromosome 14.

MYC stays switched on

The heavy-chain gene's regulatory elements are very active in B cells, so MYC is now expressed when it should be off. MYC switches on genes that push the cell through the cell cycle and build what it needs to grow.

The clone outgrows the host

One B cell with the translocation divides without stopping, and all its daughters carry the same change. The result is among the fastest-growing human tumours.

Quick checkMost sporadic Burkitt lymphomas carry no Epstein-Barr virus. How do they still arise?

Through the same MYC translocation. Virtually all endemic and sporadic tumours have t(8;14) or a variant that switches on MYC, so sporadic tumours reach the same pathway without the virus.

Key facts

  • Endemic Burkitt lymphoma peaks in children of primary school age, and boys are affected more than girls.

Endemic Burkitt lymphoma is a disease of children, more often boys. The signs follow where the tumour grows.

Course of Burkitt lymphoma

Burkitt lymphoma kills fast without treatment, but it can be cured. In Nigeria the outcome depends on reaching care early and staying on treatment.

Watch out. You might expect a tumour that killed every child Burkitt saw to be beyond cure. But sporadic Burkitt lymphoma is now usually cured. In the endemic African form long-term cure is uncommon, because poor supportive care limits how intensive treatment can be.

Quick checkIn Nigeria many children with Burkitt lymphoma still die of a tumour that can be cured. Why?

Late presentation and treatment that stops early. In Nigerian children with cancer, abandonment of treatment is common, and advanced stage at diagnosis predicts death. In the endemic form long-term cure is uncommon.

Epstein-Barr virus in Burkitt lymphoma

Key facts

  • Epstein-Barr virus enters B cells through CD21, the receptor those cells use for the complement fragment C3d.
  • In most infected B cells the virus stays latent: its DNA sits in the nucleus as a circle and no virus is made.
  • Epstein-Barr virus sits in the tumour cells of virtually every endemic Burkitt lymphoma.

Epstein-Barr virus was the first virus linked to a human tumour, and that tumour was Burkitt lymphoma.

Connect this to other specialties

Epstein-Barr virus is a herpesvirus that infects B cells through and sets up a that lasts for life. In the B cells that become activated, viral proteins drive the cell to divide.

Five steps, from the virus landing on a B cell to a tumour clone.

How Epstein-Barr virus helps cause endemic Burkitt lymphoma

  1. A viral surface glycoprotein binds CD21, which every B cell carries. That binding is why Epstein-Barr virus infects B cells.

  2. Inside the B cell the viral DNA stays as a circle in the nucleus, and most infected cells make no new virus. A minority switch to making virus, and those cells burst.

  3. Two viral proteins, LMP1 and EBNA2, drive the infected B cells to divide. LMP1 mimics , the receptor a helper T cell normally engages through CD40 ligand, but LMP1 is switched on all the time.

  4. In a healthy child, cytotoxic T cells kill the dividing infected B cells. Either nothing is felt, or the child has a self-limited attack of infectious mononucleosis.

  5. A few infected B cells switch off LMP1 and EBNA2 and survive, because T cells no longer see them. Burkitt lymphoma is thought to grow from one of these cells after a MYC translocation.

Epstein-Barr virus is found in several tumours and causes infectious mononucleosis.

Watch out. A virus found in virtually every endemic tumour looks like the whole cause. But nearly every child in this region meets Epstein-Barr virus in the first years of life, and almost none of them develops Burkitt lymphoma. The virus is a .

Malaria and the lymphoma belt

Key facts

  • Endemic Burkitt lymphoma follows the map of holoendemic falciparum malaria across equatorial Africa.
  • Each attack of malaria drives B cells to divide, and dividing B cells are where a MYC translocation can arise.
  • Children in malaria-endemic areas have weakened T-cell responses to Epstein-Barr virus, so infected B cells persist.

Endemic Burkitt lymphoma occurs where falciparum malaria is transmitted nearly all year. Repeated malaria acts on a child's B cells, and on the T cells that hold Epstein-Barr virus in check.

How repeated malaria raises the risk of Burkitt lymphoma

  1. Burkitt lymphoma follows the belt where falciparum malaria is transmitted for more than six months of the year, and it's uncommon at high altitude and in dry, seasonal areas. A child in such an area is infected many times from infancy, and each attack of malaria sets off a broad, activation of B cells.

  2. Those B cells keep dividing and keep editing their immunoglobulin genes. Every round of that editing is a chance for a break that joins MYC on chromosome 8 to an immunoglobulin gene.

  3. Malaria also suppresses the T-cell response to Epstein-Barr virus, and the amount of virus in the blood rises during an attack. Infected B cells that T cells would have killed survive instead.

Quick checkEpstein-Barr virus and malaria are both almost universal in this belt, yet Burkitt lymphoma stays an uncommon tumour. Why?

Both organisms raise the risk but can't cause the tumour on their own. The tumour also needs a MYC translocation, and that break happens by chance in one dividing B cell.

Origin of Burkitt lymphoma from the germinal centre B cell, step by step

Step 1 of 5

Naive B cell

B cells are made in the marrow. A precursor B cell carries and has no surface immunoglobulin. A naive B cell leaving the marrow has lost TdT and carries surface IgM, its antigen receptor.

Antigen starts a germinal centre

In a lymph node follicle, continued stimulation by antigen builds a germinal centre. Activated B cells move into it and divide very fast. Most of them die there by unless antigen selects them to survive.

Immunoglobulin genes are cut

Dividing germinal centre B cells mutate their immunoglobulin genes () and change their heavy-chain class (). Both processes break the DNA, and both make mistakes.

A break joins MYC

A mistaken break and rejoin can put MYC, from chromosome 8, beside an immunoglobulin gene. Many translocations in mature B-cell tumours involve the immunoglobulin genes and seem to arise from accidents like this one.

The clone keeps its markers

Every cell of the tumour comes from that one B cell, so all carry the same immunoglobulin rearrangement. Surface IgM and CD20 mark a mature B cell, and CD10 and BCL6 mark the germinal centre. Those markers let the pathologist name the cell of origin.

BonusWhy do translocations cause many B-cell lymphomas but very few T-cell lymphomas?

Germinal centre B cells cut their immunoglobulin genes during class switching and somatic hypermutation, and T cells don't. Mature T cells only very rarely carry translocations of their receptor genes.

Mechanism of MYC overexpression in Burkitt lymphoma

  1. MYC sits on the long arm of chromosome 8. In a normal cell it's switched on only in parts of the cell cycle and switched off in others.

  2. In t(8;14), breaks on the long arms of chromosomes 8 and 14 rejoin the wrong way. MYC moves to chromosome 14 beside the immunoglobulin heavy-chain gene, and part of the heavy-chain gene moves to chromosome 8.

  3. A B cell transcribes its immunoglobulin genes all the time, under very active control elements. Those elements now drive MYC too, so MYC is made at high levels, at times in the cell cycle when it should be off.

  4. MYC is a transcription factor. It switches on cyclin-dependent kinases that push the cell into the cell cycle, and genes that make amino acids, lipids and nucleotides. It also drives aerobic glycolysis.

  5. With MYC on all the time, nearly every tumour cell is dividing. Burkitt lymphoma is among the fastest-growing human tumours.

Watch out. You might expect a translocation to make a new fusion protein, as BCR-ABL does in chronic myeloid leukaemia. In Burkitt lymphoma it doesn't. The MYC protein is normal, but the cell makes too much of it, at the wrong time.

Pathogenesis of tumour lysis syndrome, step by step

Step 1 of 5

Tumour cells burst

Burkitt lymphoma is among the fastest-growing human tumours, so chemotherapy kills a great many of its cells at once. Their membranes break, and the contents of the cells pour into the plasma.

Potassium leaves the cells

Potassium inside the dying cells moves out into the plasma, and plasma potassium rises. Hyperkalaemia carries the danger of cardiac arrest.

Phosphate lowers calcium

Phosphate is the main anion inside cells, so plasma phosphate rises. Once calcium and phosphate pass their , calcium phosphate precipitates in the tissues. Calcium phosphate can also crystallise in the renal tubules and add to the kidney injury. Plasma calcium falls, and a low calcium causes tetany and seizures.

Nucleic acids become urate

DNA and RNA from the dead cells are broken down to their , adenine and guanine. Adenine is turned into hypoxanthine, and guanine into xanthine. In the liver, xanthine oxidase oxidises hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to urate. Humans lack the enzyme that breaks urate down further, so plasma urate rises.

Urate blocks the tubules

Urate dissolves poorly. A sudden load crystallises in the renal tubules and blocks them, causing acute oliguric kidney injury, and plasma creatinine rises. Failing kidneys then excrete less potassium, phosphate and urate, so each result climbs further.

Watch out. When cells burst, it's natural to expect every result to rise. But calcium falls. Dying cells release phosphate, and the extra phosphate takes calcium out of the plasma as calcium phosphate.

Key facts

  • Methotrexate blocks dihydrofolate reductase, so the cell can't make the thymidylate and purines DNA needs.

Burkitt lymphoma is treated with several cytotoxic drugs together, among them cyclophosphamide, vincristine, methotrexate, cytarabine and doxorubicin. Each one damages DNA or stops cell division at a different point, and some are also given into the CSF.

Adverse effects of cytotoxic drugs in Burkitt lymphoma

Key facts

  • Acrolein, a breakdown product of cyclophosphamide, causes haemorrhagic cystitis; hydration and mesna prevent it.
  • Vincristine's dose is limited by peripheral neuropathy and paralytic ileus, and its marrow suppression is mild.
  • Methotrexate is cleared by the kidneys, so kidney injury, as in tumour lysis, lets it build up to toxic levels.

Cytotoxic drugs also kill normal cells that divide fast, so most of them suppress the marrow and damage the lining of the gut. Each drug also has an adverse effect of its own.

Allopurinol and rasburicase in tumour lysis syndrome

Key facts

  • Allopurinol blocks xanthine oxidase, so less urate is made and the more soluble xanthine builds up.

Allopurinol and rasburicase in purine breakdown, step by step

Step 1 of 5

Nucleic acids released

Dying tumour cells break open and release their DNA and RNA. The in them, adenine and guanine, are broken down to hypoxanthine and xanthine.

Xanthine oxidase makes urate

turns hypoxanthine into xanthine, and xanthine into urate. Urate dissolves less well than either, and when a tumour lyses, so much is made that it can crystallise in the renal tubules and block them.

Allopurinol blocks the enzyme

Allopurinol is a purine analogue that inhibits xanthine oxidase, and so does its active metabolite, oxypurinol. Less urate is made, and hypoxanthine and xanthine, which dissolve more easily, build up instead.

Rasburicase adds a step

Humans lack urate oxidase, so urate is the end of their purine breakdown. Rasburicase is a recombinant urate oxidase: it turns urate into allantoin, which is much more soluble and easily excreted.

Fluids wash urate out

Intravenous fluids keep the urine flow high, so urate is carried out of the tubules before it crystallises. Fluids are given with allopurinol through treatment, as long as glomerular function isn't impaired.

  1. Where does the endemic form grow?

    Outside the lymph nodes: in the maxilla or mandible and the orbit, and now more often in the abdomen.

  2. Why is Burkitt lymphoma commoner in Nigeria than in Europe?

    The endemic form follows holoendemic malaria. EBV is in virtually every endemic tumour, and malaria weakens T-cell control of EBV-infected B cells, so they keep dividing and a MYC translocation has more chances to happen.

Tell me what you know about the starry-sky pattern.

  1. Are the macrophages part of the tumour?

    No. Tingible body macrophages are benign host cells.

  2. Is a starry sky diagnostic of Burkitt lymphoma?

    No. Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma and B-lymphoblastic leukaemia can show one too. The immunophenotype and the MYC translocation confirm Burkitt lymphoma.

Tell me about the pathogenesis of endemic Burkitt lymphoma.

Epstein-Barr virus infects B cells through CD21 and drives them to divide. Malaria weakens T-cell control, so the infected B cells keep dividing. In the germinal centre a mistake during immunoglobulin gene remodelling moves MYC next to the heavy-chain gene. MYC stays switched on, and the clone outgrows the host.

  1. What does MYC do in the cell?

    MYC is a transcription factor. It drives the cell through the cell cycle and switches on the metabolism the cell needs to grow.

  2. Does Epstein-Barr virus cause Burkitt lymphoma on its own?

    No. EBV is a cofactor. Most sporadic tumours carry no EBV, and every form needs a MYC translocation.

Microbiology and Parasitology

Tell me what you know about Epstein-Barr virus.

It's a herpesvirus. It infects B cells through CD21, the receptor for the complement fragment C3d, and then stays latent, with its DNA held in the nucleus as a circle. It causes infectious mononucleosis, and it's found in Burkitt lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, Hodgkin lymphoma and the B-cell lymphomas of immunosuppressed patients.

  1. How does Epstein-Barr virus make an infected B cell divide?

    Two viral proteins do it. LMP1 mimics CD40, the receptor a helper T cell normally engages, but it's switched on all the time, and EBNA2 switches on genes that drive growth.

  2. What stops that division in a healthy child?

    Cytotoxic T cells kill the dividing infected B cells. The child either feels nothing or has a self-limited attack of infectious mononucleosis.

  3. If almost every child here carries the virus, why do so few develop Burkitt lymphoma?

    Epstein-Barr virus is a cofactor. The tumour also needs a MYC translocation in a dividing B cell, and that break happens by chance in one cell.

  4. Which test shows the virus inside the tumour cells?

    EBER in situ hybridisation on the tissue section. In an EBV-positive tumour every neoplastic cell stains.

Tell me what you know about the part malaria plays in Burkitt lymphoma.

Endemic Burkitt lymphoma occurs in the tropics, where falciparum malaria is transmitted nearly all year. Repeated malaria drives B cells to divide, which raises the chance of a MYC translocation, and it weakens the T-cell response to Epstein-Barr virus, so infected B cells survive.

  1. Where in Africa is the tumour commonest, and where is it uncommon?

    It's commonest in an equatorial belt where malaria is transmitted for more than six months of the year. It's uncommon at high altitude and in dry areas where transmission is seasonal.

  2. Does the parasite infect the B cell itself?

    No. Plasmodium falciparum infects red cells and liver cells. Its effect on B cells is indirect, through repeated activation and through weakened T-cell control of the virus.

  3. Would a negative malaria film argue against the diagnosis?

    No. Past attacks are what raise the risk, and they leave nothing on today's film. A film says only whether the child has malaria now.

Tell me what you know about Burkitt lymphoma in HIV infection.

Burkitt lymphoma is one of the lymphomas seen in HIV, and it arises early, while the CD4 count is still preserved. It comes from germinal centre B cells that divide and mutate, with a MYC translocation. HIV is sought in every new case of Burkitt and Burkitt-like lymphoma.

  1. How do the lymphomas of advanced HIV differ?

    They follow deep T-cell loss, they often sit at unusual sites such as the central nervous system, and the tumour cells carry a virus, usually Epstein-Barr virus.

Haematology and Immunology

Tell me what you know about the immunophenotype of Burkitt lymphoma.

Burkitt lymphoma is a mature B-cell tumour: CD20 positive, with surface IgM. CD10 and BCL6 show it comes from a germinal centre B cell. It's TdT negative and BCL2 negative or weak, and Ki-67 stains over 95% of the cells.

  1. Why does Ki-67 stain nearly every cell?

    Ki-67 marks cells in the cell cycle. The MYC translocation keeps MYC switched on, so nearly every tumour cell is dividing.

  2. What does a negative TdT tell you?

    TdT is made by precursor B and T cells. A negative TdT says the tumour isn't lymphoblastic, so it isn't B-lymphoblastic leukaemia or lymphoma.

  3. The tumour is strongly BCL2 positive. What do you think now?

    Strong BCL2 argues against Burkitt lymphoma, which is BCL2 negative or weak. Think of another aggressive B-cell lymphoma, such as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma.

Tell me about the translocation in Burkitt lymphoma.

Most cases carry t(8;14): MYC on chromosome 8 moves beside the immunoglobulin heavy-chain gene on chromosome 14. Variants join MYC to the kappa gene on chromosome 2 or the lambda gene on chromosome 22. In each, immunoglobulin control elements keep MYC switched on, and MYC drives the cell into the cell cycle.

  1. Why does the break happen in a B cell?

    Germinal centre B cells cut their immunoglobulin genes during class switching and somatic hypermutation. Those breaks make mistakes, and one can join MYC to an immunoglobulin gene.

  2. How would you prove the translocation in the laboratory?

    FISH for MYC, which works even on cells that aren't dividing, or a karyotype. Strong MYC staining supports the diagnosis but doesn't prove the translocation.

By the markers, not the vacuoles. Burkitt cells are mature B cells: TdT negative, with surface IgM of one light chain. Lymphoblasts are TdT positive with no surface immunoglobulin. FISH shows a MYC rearrangement in Burkitt lymphoma.

  1. Does CD10 or BCL2 help?

    No. Both tumours can be CD10 positive and BCL2 negative or weak.

  2. In a lymph node, which other lymphoma can look like Burkitt lymphoma?

    Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. Its cells are larger and more varied, it can be strongly BCL2 positive, and some cases show a starry sky.

Chemical Pathology

Tell me what you know about tumour lysis syndrome in Burkitt lymphoma.

When chemotherapy kills many tumour cells at once, they release potassium, phosphate and nucleic acids. Plasma potassium, phosphate and urate rise, and calcium falls. Urate crystallises in the renal tubules and causes acute kidney injury. Burkitt lymphoma is among the highest-risk tumours.

  1. Why does the calcium fall?

    Dying cells release phosphate. When calcium and phosphate pass their solubility product, calcium phosphate precipitates in the tissues, so plasma calcium falls.

  2. Where does the urate come from?

    From the nucleic acids of the dead cells. Their purines are released: adenine is turned into hypoxanthine, and guanine into xanthine. Xanthine oxidase in the liver oxidises hypoxanthine to xanthine and xanthine to urate, and humans can't break urate down any further.

  3. Why does the creatinine rise?

    A sudden load of urate crystallises in the renal tubules and blocks them, causing acute oliguric kidney injury. The failing kidneys then excrete less potassium, phosphate and urate, so those results climb further.

Tell me what you know about lactate dehydrogenase in Burkitt lymphoma.

LDH is found in many tissues and leaks from damaged cells, so it's a non-specific marker of cell damage. In lymphoma it can be very high, and it's used as a tumour marker to follow the disease.

  1. How is it used during and after treatment?

    A raised LDH that falls shows the tumour responding to treatment. Measured again in remission, a new rise can show the tumour coming back.

  2. What else can raise the LDH?

    Myocardial infarction, shock and megaloblastic anaemia can raise it a lot. Infectious mononucleosis and viral hepatitis raise it less. Haemolysis or late separation raises it falsely, in the tube.

  3. Why can the urate be high before any treatment?

    Burkitt lymphoma cells divide and die very fast, so the turnover of their nucleic acids raises the urate even before chemotherapy starts.

Tell me what you know about pseudohyperkalaemia.

Pseudohyperkalaemia is a potassium raised in the tube, not in the patient. Potassium leaks out of cells after the blood is taken, from haemolysis or a delay before the plasma is separated. The potassium result isn't reported.

  1. Which other results does a haemolysed sample raise?

    Phosphate and LDH, because red cells that burst in the tube release them too.

  2. How do you tell it from tumour lysis?

    Look at the sample first. A haemolysed or late-separated sample can't give a valid potassium, so the potassium isn't reported and a fresh sample is taken. In tumour lysis the urate, calcium and creatinine may also be abnormal, but laboratory tumour lysis needs only two abnormal results.

Pharmacology

Tell me what you know about the drugs used to treat Burkitt lymphoma.

Burkitt lymphoma is treated with combination chemotherapy: cyclophosphamide, vincristine and methotrexate, with cytarabine and doxorubicin, and methotrexate or cytarabine into the CSF. Nearly every tumour cell is dividing, so the tumour is very sensitive to them.

  1. How does cyclophosphamide kill tumour cells?

    It's a prodrug. Liver cytochrome P450 enzymes convert it to metabolites that break down to phosphoramide mustard, which cross-links the two strands of DNA so the cell can't copy it. It's cell cycle–nonspecific, but dividing cells are the most sensitive.

  2. Why is methotrexate also given into the CSF?

    Few drugs in the blood reach the CSF. Brain capillaries have no pores for drugs and carry drug export pumps, so the CNS is a sanctuary site where tumour cells escape. High intravenous doses of methotrexate get in better, but methotrexate, alone or with cytarabine, is still given intrathecally to prevent or treat CNS disease.

  3. What harm does vincristine do?

    Its dose-limiting toxicity is nerve damage: a peripheral sensory neuropathy, and autonomic damage with constipation or paralytic ileus. It can also cause SIADH. Its marrow suppression is mild.

Tell me what you know about the drugs that prevent tumour lysis syndrome.

Intravenous fluids, allopurinol and electrolyte replacement are the mainstay. Allopurinol blocks xanthine oxidase, so less urate is made. Rasburicase, a recombinant urate oxidase, turns urate into soluble allantoin, but G6PD is tested before it's given.

  1. Why is G6PD tested before rasburicase?

    Rasburicase makes hydrogen peroxide as it turns urate into allantoin. G6PD is the red cell's only source of the NADPH that makes reduced glutathione, so deficient cells can't neutralise the peroxide. Severe haemolytic anaemia and methaemoglobinaemia follow.

  2. Why do the hypoxanthine and xanthine that build up under allopurinol do less harm than urate?

    Both dissolve more easily than urate, so they're less likely to crystallise in the renal tubules.

    A plasma protein, such as CRP or fibrinogen, that the liver makes in larger amounts when cytokines signal inflammation.

    The formation of antibodies against antigens on transfused red cells that the patient's own red cells lack.

    A mild, normocytic anaemia of long-lasting infection, inflammation or cancer. Hepcidin holds iron in macrophages, so the marrow is short of iron while stores are full.

    Sodium plus potassium, minus chloride and bicarbonate, in the plasma. It stands for the anions that aren't measured, and it widens when an acid such as lactate replaces bicarbonate.

    A plump, activated macrophage in an Aschoff body, with abundant cytoplasm and chromatin condensed into a slender, wavy ribbon down the middle of the nucleus.
    Apoptosis
    Regulated cell death: the cell switches on enzymes that dismantle it from within, in pieces that are eaten without inflammation.
    Aschoff body
    A focus of inflammation pathognomonic of acute rheumatic fever: T lymphocytes, scattered plasma cells and Anitschkow cells around a zone of fibrinoid necrosis.
    ASO titre (anti-streptolysin O titre)
    The level of antibody against streptolysin O in serum. A raised titre shows a recent group A streptococcal infection.
    Atherosclerosis
    Disease of large and medium-sized arteries in which raised plaques, a lipid core under a fibrous cap, form in the intima and narrow the lumen.
    Atrophy
    A decrease in the size of an organ or tissue, from smaller cells, fewer cells, or both.
    Autosplenectomy
    Loss of the spleen to repeated infarcts, which shrink it to a small nubbin of fibrous tissue. In sickle cell disease it's complete by adulthood.
    Avascular necrosis (osteonecrosis)
    Infarction of bone and marrow. Under a joint, a wedge of dead bone forms and can collapse, as in the femoral head.
    Beta-haemolysis (β-haemolysis)
    Complete lysis of the red cells in blood agar around a bacterial colony, leaving a clear zone. Alpha-haemolysis is partial lysis, with a green zone.

    Sudden intravascular haemolysis in falciparum malaria, with haemoglobin in the urine turning it dark, and acute kidney injury.
    Blood schizonticide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the asexual parasites inside red cells, the stage that causes the illness. Every drug that treats malaria is one.
    C-peptide
    A peptide the pancreas releases along with insulin. It's raised when the patient's own pancreas makes too much insulin, and suppressed when insulin is injected.
    Caseous necrosis (caseation)
    Necrosis in which dead tissue loses all structure and becomes soft, granular and cheese-like. Down the microscope it has no nuclei and no preserved architecture.
    Caspase
    One of a family of enzymes that carry out apoptosis.
    CD21
    A receptor on B cells for the complement fragment C3d. Epstein-Barr virus binds CD21 to get into the B cell.
    CD40
    A receptor on B cells. A helper T cell engages it through CD40 ligand to make the B cell divide. LMP1 of Epstein-Barr virus mimics CD40 and is switched on all the time.
    Cell cycle–nonspecific drug
    A cytotoxic drug that kills cells whether they're cycling or resting in G0, such as cyclophosphamide or doxorubicin. Cycling cells are still more sensitive to it.
    Cell cycle–specific drug
    A cytotoxic drug that kills cells only while they pass through one phase of the cell cycle: methotrexate and cytarabine in S phase, vincristine in M phase.
    Cell-mediated immunity
    Defence carried out by T cells. Sensitised T cells activate macrophages so that they can kill the organisms they have phagocytosed.
    Cinchonism
    Tinnitus, headache, nausea, dizziness, flushing and blurred vision caused by quinine at treatment doses. Quinine comes from the bark of the cinchona tree.
    Class switching
    A change in the heavy-chain constant region a B cell uses, for example from IgM to IgG. It cuts and rejoins the heavy-chain gene in the germinal centre.
    Coagulative necrosis
    Necrosis in which the dead cells keep their outline for some days, so the tissue's architecture can still be seen.
    Cofactor
    A cause that helps a tumour arise but can't produce it alone. Epstein-Barr virus is a cofactor in endemic Burkitt lymphoma.
    Commissural fusion
    Scarring that joins neighbouring valve leaflets or cusps at their edges, so the valve can't open fully. It's a sign of past inflammation.
    Counter-regulatory hormones
    Hormones that raise plasma glucose and oppose insulin: glucagon, adrenaline, cortisol and growth hormone.
    Culture-negative endocarditis
    Infective endocarditis in which no organism grows from blood cultures, usually because antibiotics were given first or the organism is hard to grow.
    Cyclooxygenase (COX)
    The enzyme that turns arachidonic acid into prostaglandins and, in platelets, thromboxane. Aspirin and other NSAIDs block it, which lowers inflammation, pain and fever.
    Cytochrome P450
    A family of liver enzymes that break down many drugs, warfarin among them. A drug that inhibits these enzymes raises the level of other drugs; a drug that induces them lowers it.
    Dactylitis (hand-foot syndrome)
    Painful swelling of the hands and feet caused by infarcts of their small bones. It's often the first presentation of sickle cell disease.
    Dihydrofolate reductase
    The enzyme that reduces dihydrofolate back to tetrahydrofolate, the active form of folate used to make DNA. Proguanil blocks the malaria parasite's own form of it.
    Duffy antigen (DARC)
    A chemokine receptor on the red cell surface. Plasmodium vivax uses it to enter the red cell, and most people of West African origin lack it.
    Dystrophic calcification
    Calcium deposited in dead or damaged tissue while the blood calcium is normal.
    EBER (EBV-encoded small RNA)
    Small RNA molecules made in large amounts by Epstein-Barr virus in a latently infected cell. A stain for EBER on a tissue section shows which cells carry the virus.
    Encapsulated bacteria
    Bacteria wrapped in a polysaccharide capsule that resists phagocytosis, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b and Neisseria meningitidis. Antibody against the capsule lets phagocytes take them up.
    Endoperoxide bridge
    The bridge of two linked oxygen atoms in the artemisinin molecule. Iron is thought to split it, and the free radicals released kill the malaria parasite.
    Epithelioid macrophage
    An activated macrophage with abundant cytoplasm and an elongated nucleus. These cells form the wall of the granuloma.
    Erythema marginatum
    A red, ring-shaped rash on the skin, one of the major Jones criteria for acute rheumatic fever.
    Euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis
    Diabetic ketoacidosis with a plasma glucose that's normal or only a little raised. SGLT2 inhibitors can cause it in patients who are very short of insulin.
    Extranodal
    Arising outside the lymph nodes, in an organ such as the jaw, bowel or kidney. Burkitt lymphoma usually arises at extranodal sites.
    Extravascular haemolysis
    Destruction of red cells by macrophages, rather than inside the blood vessels. It causes jaundice and pigment gallstones.
    Fat necrosis
    Death of fat cells, from released lipases or from injury, with chalky white deposits where fatty acids bind calcium.
    Fc receptor
    A receptor on macrophages, neutrophils and other leucocytes that binds the tail (Fc part) of an antibody, so the cell can engulf or attack what the antibody has coated.
    Ferritin
    The protein that stores iron. Its plasma level usually follows the iron stores, but it's an acute-phase protein, so inflammation raises it too.
    Fetal warfarin syndrome
    Birth defects, chiefly abnormal bone formation, after a fetus is exposed to warfarin early in pregnancy. Warfarin crosses the placenta and blocks the carboxylation of fetal bone proteins.
    Fibrinoid necrosis
    Necrosis of a vessel wall where immune complexes or leaked plasma proteins are deposited, staining bright pink.
    Fluorescence in situ hybridisation (FISH)
    A test that binds fluorescent DNA probes to chosen genes in a cell's nucleus. A probe pair can show a gene broken apart or moved to a new chromosome, even in cells that aren't dividing.
    Flow cytometry
    A test that reads fluorescent antibodies bound to cells in suspension, cell by cell. It works on blood, marrow aspirate or disaggregated lymph node tissue.
    Free radical
    A molecule with an unpaired electron, which reacts with and damages lipids, proteins and DNA.
    Fructosamine
    Glucose bound to plasma proteins, mostly albumin. It reflects glucose control over the last few weeks, and it's used when HbA1c can't be trusted.
    Functional asplenia (hyposplenism)
    A spleen that's present but can't do its work of filtering the blood and clearing bacteria. In sickle cell disease it starts in early childhood, while the spleen is still large.
    Gametocide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the sexual forms of the parasite, the gametocytes, so they can't pass the infection on to a mosquito.
    Gametocyte
    The male or female sexual form of Plasmodium in red cells. A biting mosquito takes gametocytes up, and the cycle goes on in the mosquito.
    Gangrene
    A clinical term for necrosis of a limb or part, usually from loss of blood supply.
    Germinal centre
    The pale centre of a lymphoid follicle, where activated B cells divide fast and switch and mutate their immunoglobulin genes. Burkitt lymphoma arises from a germinal centre B cell.
    Gluconeogenesis
    The making of new glucose, mainly in the liver, from sources such as lactate and amino acids. Insulin suppresses it, and metformin reduces it.
    GLUT4
    The glucose transporter of muscle and fat cells. Insulin moves it from inside the cell to the cell membrane, so these cells take up glucose.
    Glycation
    Binding of glucose to a protein without an enzyme. The higher the blood glucose, the faster it happens.
    Granuloma
    A compact collection of epithelioid macrophages, often with multinucleate giant cells, ringed by lymphocytes.
    Growth fraction
    The share of a tumour's cells that are actively passing through the cell cycle. In Burkitt lymphoma it's nearly all of them.
    HACEK group
    Haemophilus, Actinobacillus, Cardiobacterium, Eikenella and Kingella: commensals of the mouth that are less common causes of infective endocarditis.
    Haemoglobinopathy
    An inherited disorder caused by a mutation that changes the structure of a globin chain, such as sickle haemoglobin (Hb S) or Hb C.
    Haemosiderin
    A golden-brown pigment of stored iron, seen where red cells have broken down or iron is in excess.
    Haemozoin (malarial pigment)
    A brown-black pigment the parasite leaves from the haemoglobin it digests. Ruptured red cells release it, and macrophages in the spleen, liver and marrow take it up.
    Haptoglobin
    A plasma protein that binds free haemoglobin. The pair is removed from the circulation, so the haptoglobin level falls in haemolysis.
    Haemoglobin electrophoresis
    A test that moves haemoglobins along a strip in an electric field, so each kind shows as a band at its own position.
    Heart failure cell
    A macrophage in the alveoli laden with haemosiderin from red cells that leaked out of congested lung capillaries.
    Hepcidin
    A hormone that keeps iron inside macrophages and blocks its absorption from the gut. Inflammation raises it, which starves the marrow of iron.
    Heterophile antibody
    An antibody made in infectious mononucleosis that agglutinates sheep red cells. The Monospot test detects it.
    Holoendemic malaria
    Malaria transmission that runs almost all year, so children are bitten by infected mosquitoes again and again from infancy.
    Howell-Jolly body
    A small remnant of DNA inside a red cell. Howell-Jolly bodies show on the blood film when the spleen has stopped working.
    High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)
    A test that passes haemoglobin through a column. Each kind leaves at its own time, so the machine names it and measures its share of the total.
    HRP2 (histidine-rich protein 2)
    A protein made by P. falciparum and no other species, and the antigen most malaria rapid diagnostic tests detect.
    Hyaline arteriolosclerosis
    Thickening of an arteriole's wall by glassy, pink, structureless material leaked from the plasma, which narrows the lumen. Hypertension, age and diabetes mellitus cause it.
    Hyperplasia
    An increase in the number of cells in a tissue, in cells that can divide.
    Hypersplenism
    An enlarged spleen with a fall in at least one blood cell line while the bone marrow works normally.
    Hypertrophy
    An increase in the size of cells, and so of the organ, without more cells.
    Hypnozoite
    A dormant liver form of P. vivax and P. ovale. Hypnozoites can wake weeks or years later and cause a relapse.
    Hypoglycaemia unawareness
    Loss of the autonomic warning signs of a low glucose after frequent episodes of hypoglycaemia, so the patient doesn't act until confusion or coma sets in.
    Hypoxia
    Too little oxygen reaching the cells.
    Immunohistochemistry
    Staining a tissue section with labelled antibodies, so cells that carry a given protein turn brown. The tissue's architecture is kept.
    Immunophenotype
    The set of proteins a cell carries, shown with labelled antibodies. In a lymphoma it tells you which kind of lymphocyte the tumour copies, and at which stage.
    Impaired fasting glucose (IFG)
    A fasting venous plasma glucose from 6.1 up to 6.9 mmol/L, with a 2-hour OGTT glucose below 7.8. A stage between normal and diabetes mellitus.
    Impaired glucose tolerance (IGT)
    A 2-hour OGTT venous plasma glucose from 7.8 up to 11.1 mmol/L, with a fasting glucose below 7.0. Some people with it go on to develop diabetes mellitus.
    Incretin
    A gut hormone released after a meal that boosts insulin release from the β cells. GLP-1 and GIP are the two main incretins, and DPP-4 breaks them down.
    Infarct
    An area of ischaemic necrosis caused by loss of the blood supply, in sickle cell disease usually from vessels blocked by sickled cells.
    International normalised ratio (INR)
    The prothrombin time corrected for the thromboplastin reagent used, so a result means the same in any laboratory. A normal INR is about 1.
    Insulin resistance
    Failure of target tissues, mainly the liver, skeletal muscle and fat, to respond normally to insulin.
    Insulitis
    Infiltration of the pancreatic islets by lymphocytes, mainly T cells, while they destroy the β cells in type 1 diabetes.
    Intermittent preventive therapy
    Treatment doses of an antimalarial given at set times to people at high risk, such as pregnant women, whether or not they're infected.
    Intrathecal
    Injected into the CSF, so a drug reaches tumour cells in the central nervous system that drugs in the blood barely reach.
    Iron chelation
    Treatment with a drug that binds iron tightly so it can leave the body in urine or faeces. It treats the iron overload of repeated blood transfusion.
    Irreversibly sickled cell
    A red cell whose membrane has been damaged by many cycles of sickling, so it stays sickled even when it's carrying oxygen.
    Ischaemia
    Too little blood reaching a tissue: less oxygen, and less of everything else the blood brings and takes away.
    Karyolysis
    A nucleus fading away as its DNA is broken down.
    Karyorrhexis
    A pyknotic nucleus breaking into fragments.
    Karyotype
    A picture of all a cell's chromosomes, laid out in pairs. It needs dividing cells, so the cells are grown in culture first.
    ATP-sensitive potassium channel
    A potassium channel in the β cell membrane that closes when ATP rises. Closing it depolarises the cell and leads to insulin release. Sulfonylureas close it directly.
    Diabetic ketoacidosis
    Metabolic acidosis from ketone bodies, which the liver makes from fatty acids when there's too little insulin to stop fat breaking down.
    Ki-67
    A nuclear protein present in cells that are in the cell cycle. A Ki-67 stain gives the share of tumour cells dividing, called the proliferation index.
    Kussmaul breathing
    Deep, sighing breathing in metabolic acidosis. It blows off carbon dioxide, which lessens the fall in pH.
    Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)
    An enzyme that turns lactate into pyruvate and back. It's found in many tissues and leaks into the plasma from damaged cells, so a raised level shows cell damage but not where.
    Lactic acidosis
    A high anion gap metabolic acidosis from lactate building up in the blood. Its commonest cause is tissue short of oxygen, from poor perfusion, hypoxia or severe anaemia.
    Lancefield group
    A letter (A, B, D and others) given to a streptococcus by the C carbohydrate antigen in its cell wall.
    Langhans giant cell
    A multinucleate cell formed when macrophages fuse. Its nuclei sit in a horseshoe around the edge of the cell.
    Latency I
    The most restricted pattern of Epstein-Barr virus gene expression, with EBNA1 and the EBER RNAs and little else. Burkitt lymphoma cells show this pattern.
    Latent infection
    An infection in which the viral DNA stays inside the cell and makes few or no new virus particles. Epstein-Barr virus keeps its DNA in the nucleus as a circle.
    Light chain restriction
    Expression of only kappa or only lambda light chain by a population of B cells. Light chain restriction shows the cells are one clone: each B cell makes one light chain, but a normal, polyclonal population carries both, kappa more often than lambda.
    Lipofuscin
    A yellow-brown 'wear and tear' pigment of oxidised lipid, building up in long-lived cells with age.
    Lipohypertrophy
    A swelling of fatty tissue under the skin where insulin is injected into the same site again and again. Insulin is absorbed unevenly from it.
    Liquefactive necrosis
    Necrosis in which enzymes digest the dead tissue into a liquid.
    M protein
    A major virulence factor of group A streptococcus: a wall protein that blocks complement activation and protects the organism from phagocytosis.
    Malignant otitis externa
    Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection of the external ear canal that burrows into the mastoid bone, mostly in older people with diabetes mellitus.
    Merozoite
    The form of Plasmodium released when an infected liver cell or red cell bursts. Each merozoite invades a red cell.
    Metabolic syndrome
    A cluster of central obesity, high triglyceride, low HDL cholesterol, raised blood pressure and raised fasting glucose, linked by insulin resistance.
    Metaplasia
    The replacement of one mature cell type by another, better able to stand a particular stress.
    Metastatic calcification
    Calcium deposited in normal tissue because the blood calcium is high.
    Methaemoglobinaemia
    Haemoglobin in the blood with its iron oxidised from the Fe2+ to the Fe3+ state. Drugs such as primaquine can cause it, and the patient looks cyanosed.
    Microalbuminuria
    A urine albumin of 30 to 300 mg a day, too little for overt proteinuria. In diabetes mellitus it's one of the earliest signs of nephropathy.
    Diabetic microangiopathy
    Disease of small blood vessels in diabetes mellitus: capillary basement membranes become thick, yet the capillaries leak plasma proteins.
    Molecular mimicry
    An immune response to a microbe that also attacks the body's own tissue, because a microbial antigen looks like a self antigen.
    Mucormycosis (zygomycosis)
    Infection with moulds such as Rhizopus and Mucor, whose hyphae have no septa, branch at right angles and invade blood vessel walls.
    Multidrug-resistant (MDR)
    Resistant to antibiotics from several different classes at once, such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus.
    Mural thrombus
    A thrombus attached to the wall of a heart chamber or of the aorta.
    Mycotic aneurysm
    An aneurysm caused by bacterial infection of an artery wall, where an infected embolus has lodged.
    Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase (the sodium pump)
    The membrane pump that moves sodium out of a cell and potassium in, using ATP. Digoxin inhibits it in heart muscle, and potassium competes with digoxin for it.
    Necrosis
    Death of cells in a living tissue, with their membranes breaking and their contents leaking out, which brings in inflammation.
    Necrotising granuloma
    A granuloma whose centre has died. In tuberculosis the dead centre is caseous.
    Nephrosclerosis
    Diffuse scarring of the kidney from narrowed arteries and arterioles, which leaves a finely granular surface and a thin cortex.
    Neurogenic bladder
    A bladder that doesn't empty properly because the nerves that control it are damaged. In diabetes mellitus autonomic neuropathy causes it, and urine left standing lets bacteria multiply.
    Neuroglycopenia
    Brain dysfunction from too little glucose: faintness, dizziness and confusion, which can progress to coma.
    Nodular glomerulosclerosis (Kimmelstiel-Wilson lesion)
    Ball-like nodules of laminated matrix at the edge of the glomerulus, almost pathognomonic of diabetes mellitus.
    Non-Hodgkin lymphoma
    Any malignant tumour of lymphocytes other than Hodgkin lymphoma. Most arise from B cells, and Burkitt lymphoma is one of them.
    Oncogene
    A gene that drives cell growth and division when it's mutated or overexpressed. MYC is the oncogene switched on in Burkitt lymphoma.
    Opsonisation
    Coating of a microbe with antibody or complement so phagocytes can recognise and ingest it.
    Osmotic diuresis
    A large flow of urine caused by a solute, such as glucose, that stays in the renal tubule and holds water with it.
    Osteomyelitis
    Infection of bone and its marrow. In sickle cell disease bacteria seed bone already damaged by infarction.
    P-selectin
    An adhesion molecule that helps sickle cells stick to the walls of blood vessels, one step in vaso-occlusion.
    Pancarditis
    Inflammation of all three layers of the heart: the pericardium, the myocardium and the endocardium, including the valves.
    Pancytopenia
    A fall in all three blood cell lines: red cells, white cells and platelets.
    Papillary necrosis (necrotising papillitis)
    Death of the renal papillae, the tips of the medullary pyramids, as a form of acute pyelonephritis. Diabetes mellitus, urinary obstruction and sickle cell anaemia are its main settings.
    Paraprotein
    One immunoglobulin made in excess by a single clone of B cells. Serum protein electrophoresis shows it as a narrow, dense band, usually in the gamma region.
    Parasitaemia
    Malaria parasites in the circulating blood, counted on a film as the share of red cells infected or the number per microlitre.
    Penicillin-binding protein (PBP)
    A bacterial enzyme that cross-links the peptide chains of peptidoglycan, which gives the cell wall its strength. Beta-lactam antibiotics bind its active site and block it.
    Pigment gallstone
    A gallstone made of calcium bilirubinate. It forms when bile carries a high load of unconjugated bilirubin, as in chronic haemolysis.
    pLDH (Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase)
    An enzyme of the malaria parasite, detected by some rapid diagnostic tests. pLDH tests turn negative within days of cure.
    Polyclonal
    Coming from many different B-cell clones at once, rather than from one. Malaria and early Epstein-Barr virus infection both drive polyclonal B-cell activation.
    Polyclonal response
    Immunoglobulins made by many B-cell clones at once, as in chronic infection. Serum protein electrophoresis shows it as a broad, diffuse rise in the gamma region.
    Polymerisation of Hb S
    Deoxygenated sickle haemoglobin molecules binding one another into long fibres. The fibres distort the red cell into a sickle shape.
    Polymicrobial infection
    Infection with more than one organism at the same time. Infection that spreads into tissue from an open wound is often polymicrobial.
    Polyol (sorbitol) pathway
    Conversion of glucose to sorbitol by aldose reductase. It uses up NADPH, so glutathione can't be regenerated and the cell is left open to oxidative stress.
    PPAR-γ
    Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma, a nuclear receptor in muscle, fat and liver that controls genes for glucose and fat handling. Pioglitazone activates it.
    Prodrug
    A drug given in an inactive form that the body converts into the active drug. Cyclophosphamide is one: liver cytochrome P450 enzymes activate it.
    Prothrombin time (PT)
    The time citrated plasma takes to clot after thromboplastin and calcium are added. It tests the extrinsic and common clotting pathways.
    Pseudohyperkalaemia
    A potassium result raised in the tube, not in the patient: potassium leaks out of cells after the blood is taken, as in a haemolysed sample or one left unseparated.
    Pseudohypoglycaemia
    A falsely low glucose result, because blood cells kept using glucose in a tube with no fluoride before the plasma was separated.
    Purine
    A nitrogen-containing base of DNA and RNA: adenine or guanine. In humans, purines that aren't reused are broken down to urate.
    Pyknosis
    A nucleus that has shrunk and stains dark, as its chromatin condenses.
    Pyuria
    Neutrophils in the urine, which can make it look turbid. It's a sign of urinary infection such as acute pyelonephritis.
    Radical cure
    Treatment that clears malaria parasites from both the blood and the liver, so P. vivax or P. ovale can't relapse. It needs a blood-stage drug plus primaquine or tafenoquine.
    Recrudescence
    The return of malaria from blood-stage parasites that treatment didn't clear. A relapse is different: it comes from dormant liver forms.
    Renal threshold for glucose
    The plasma glucose, about 10 mmol/L, above which the renal tubules can't reabsorb all the filtered glucose, so glucose appears in the urine.
    Ischaemia–reperfusion injury
    Further injury to ischaemic tissue when its blood supply comes back.
    Reticulocyte
    A young red cell, just out of the marrow, that still holds RNA. The reticulocyte count shows how well the marrow is making red cells.
    Reye syndrome
    An illness of children and adolescents linked to taking aspirin during a viral infection, with or without fever. Aspirin isn't given to children or adolescents with a viral illness.
    Ribonucleotide reductase
    The enzyme that turns ribonucleotides into the deoxyribonucleotides a cell needs to copy its DNA. Blocking it stops dividing cells in the S phase of the cell cycle.
    Ring abscess
    An abscess in the tissue around a valve ring, where an infective vegetation has eroded into the myocardium beneath it.
    Ring haemorrhage
    A small haemorrhage in the brain's white matter arranged as a ring around a small vessel, seen at autopsy in cerebral malaria.
    Rouleaux
    Red cells stacked like a pile of coins. Fibrinogen and immunoglobulins cause the stacking, and the stacks settle fast, raising the ESR.
    Sanctuary site
    A tissue that drugs in the blood reach poorly, such as the brain and the testes, so tumour cells there can survive treatment.
    Schizont
    A Plasmodium form whose nucleus has divided many times. It breaks up into merozoites when the liver cell or red cell bursts.
    Schüffner's dots
    Fine dots in a red cell infected with P. vivax or P. ovale, seen on a well-stained Giemsa film as the parasite matures.
    Senescence
    A permanent stop to cell division that cells reach with age.
    Sequestration
    Red cells carrying maturing P. falciparum sticking to the endothelium of small vessels, through the parasite protein PfEMP1, so they leave the circulating blood and block the capillaries.
    SGLT2 (sodium-glucose co-transporter 2)
    The transporter in the proximal tubule that reabsorbs most of the glucose filtered by the kidney. SGLT2 inhibitors block it, so glucose is lost in the urine.
    Siderofibrotic nodule
    A small nodule of fibrous tissue holding iron pigment and calcium, found in the scarred spleen of sickle cell disease beside vessels full of sickled cells.
    Hepatic sinusoidal lymphocytosis
    Lymphocytes filling the sinusoids of the liver on biopsy. It supports a diagnosis of tropical splenomegaly syndrome but isn't specific to it.
    Solubility product
    The highest product of two ion concentrations that a solution can hold. Above it, the salt comes out of solution, as calcium phosphate does when plasma phosphate is high.
    Somatic hypermutation
    Random mutation of the variable-region genes of immunoglobulin in dividing germinal centre B cells. Cells whose new antibody binds antigen better are selected to survive.
    Pitting
    The spleen's removal of inclusions, such as Howell-Jolly bodies and Heinz bodies, from red cells it then lets go back into the blood.
    Splenic sequestration
    Sudden pooling of sickled red cells in the spleen, which enlarges while the haemoglobin falls. Seen mostly in infants with sickle cell disease.
    Splenomegaly
    Enlargement of the spleen. A massive spleen can reach the right iliac fossa.
    Sporozoite
    The form of Plasmodium that an infected female Anopheles mosquito injects when it bites. Sporozoites travel in the blood to the liver.
    Starry-sky pattern
    Pale tingible body macrophages scattered through sheets of dark tumour cells, seen at low power. Typical of Burkitt lymphoma, but not specific to it.
    Steatosis (fatty change)
    Triglyceride building up inside cells, seen most in the liver.
    Streptolysin O
    An oxygen-labile toxin of group A streptococcus that lyses red and white cells. It's antigenic, so infection is followed by anti-streptolysin O antibodies.
    Sydenham chorea
    A neurological disorder of acute rheumatic fever with involuntary, purposeless, rapid movements.
    Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT)
    A DNA polymerase made by precursor B and T cells, before they mature. A positive TdT stain marks a lymphoblast.
    Tingible body macrophage
    A benign macrophage that has eaten the nuclear debris of apoptotic cells. In Burkitt lymphoma these macrophages form the pale stars of the starry sky.
    Tissue schizonticide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the parasites developing or lying dormant in the liver, such as primaquine.
    Chromosomal translocation
    A break in two chromosomes with an exchange of the broken pieces. In Burkitt lymphoma, t(8;14) moves MYC on chromosome 8 next to the immunoglobulin heavy-chain gene on chromosome 14.
    Trophozoite
    The growing stage of Plasmodium inside a red cell. On a stained film the young trophozoite looks like a ring.
    Tumour lysis syndrome
    The chemical upset when many tumour cells die at once, usually as treatment starts: urate, potassium and phosphate rise, calcium falls, and the kidneys can fail.
    Tumour marker
    A substance in the blood that a tumour makes or releases. In some tumours its level relates to the tumour's mass or spread, and a fall or a new rise can follow treatment and recurrence.
    Type II hypersensitivity
    Tissue injury caused by IgG or IgM antibodies that bind antigens fixed on cells or in the tissue matrix.
    Type III hypersensitivity
    Tissue injury caused by antigen-antibody (immune) complexes that deposit in vessel walls or glomeruli and activate complement.
    Unconjugated bilirubin
    Bilirubin that the liver hasn't yet joined to glucuronate. It isn't water-soluble, so it travels in the plasma bound to albumin and doesn't pass into the urine.
    Urobilinogen
    A colourless product that gut bacteria make from bilirubin. Some is absorbed and sent back into bile by the liver, and a little passes in the urine.
    Vaso-occlusion
    Blockage of small blood vessels by sickled red cells, which causes ischaemia, pain and infarction in the tissue they supply.
    Vegetation
    A thrombus on a heart valve. It may be sterile, or hold organisms, as in infective endocarditis.
    Verrucae (rheumatic vegetations)
    Small, warty vegetations in a row along the lines of closure of a valve in acute rheumatic fever, made of fibrinoid necrosis and fibrin.
    Villous lymphocyte
    A lymphocyte with fine hair-like projections from its cytoplasm. Villous lymphocytes in the blood suggest splenic marginal zone lymphoma.
    Virchow's triad
    The three causes of thrombosis: endothelial injury, stasis or turbulent blood flow, and hypercoagulability of the blood.
    Viridans streptococci
    Alpha-haemolytic streptococci that are normal flora of the mouth. They're the usual cause of subacute infective endocarditis on a damaged valve.
    Vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR)
    The liver enzyme that turns used vitamin K epoxide back into active vitamin K, so clotting factors can go on being carboxylated. Warfarin blocks it. Its gene is VKORC1.
    Whipple's triad
    A low plasma glucose, neuroglycopenic symptoms, and relief of the symptoms when the glucose is raised. Together they show that symptoms come from hypoglycaemia.
    Xanthine oxidase
    The liver enzyme that oxidises hypoxanthine to xanthine, and xanthine to urate. Allopurinol inhibits it.
